COMMENTARY

Confucius/Legge
All things owe their inception to the vast and originating power of The Dynamic. It contains all the meaning of the word: Heaven. Clouds move, rain falls, and the myriad things appear in their created forms. The sages comprehend the link between the end and the beginning. They understand how the changes of the six lines of the hexagram are accomplished, each in its season, and with this knowledge they ascend toward Heaven as though mounted on six dragons. The intent of The Dynamic is to transform everything so that it reflects its correct nature as originally conceived by the mind of Heaven. Thereafter, this great harmony is preserved in union and firm correctness. The sage appears aloft, high above all things, and the myriad states are harmoniously united.
Legge
For the Chinese, the dragon has been from the earliest times a symbol of dignity, wisdom, sovereignty and sagehood. It is the symbol of the superior man, and especially the "great man," exhibiting all the virtues and attributes of Heaven. Although the dragon's home is in the water, it can disport itself on land, and also fly through the air.
Note
The sage rules in the world of men as Heaven rules nature. He sees the connection between the end and the beginning as the law of cause and effect in the operations of nature and human affairs. The various steps in that course are symbolized by the lines of the hexagram, and the ideal sage, conducting his ideal government, is represented as driving through the sky in a carriage drawn by six dragons.

NOTES & PARAPHRASES

Note
Judgment: The Dynamic is the life force itself. The Superior Man tirelessly furthers the Great Work of Transformation. The image of a dragon appears in every line of this hexagram, except the third. Like most symbols, the dragon has both a positive and a negative aspect. In Western myth, it is usually an adversary which the hero must conquer before he can obtain a treasure or often, a captive maiden. The Chinese, on the other hand, regard the dragon positively. Blofeld comments: In China, the dragon has always been regarded as a highly admirable creature of celestial origin. Dragons provide rain; make rivers run and rule the ocean. The European dragon is clearly of another species. This seeming anomaly may say more about how east and west perceive the forces of nature, rather than refer to any true differences in the forces perceived. Wilhelm compares the dragon to the electrical energy within a thunderstorm -- as lightning it can destroy us, but in the form of electricity it can be harnessed to do useful work. A dragon is nothing if not a huge serpent, and this suggests the idea of the "serpent power," or Kundalini energy which when aroused in the human body has been likened to a sudden jolt of electricity running up from the base of the spine to the top of the head. The Kundalini force is equated in turn with sexual energy -- dynamic power which ensures the continuance of all but the most primitive of living organisms. Without the powerful energy of sexuality, life as we know it could not exist. When the dragon remains unconquered in the cave-like depths of the unconscious, the life force autonomously rules our lives and we become passive vehicles for random desires and appetites. This "electricity" will flow wherever it finds a circuit of least resistance, and under these conditions an individual's life is largely "created" by chance and circumstance. When one begins the Work, the task of Individuation, one assumes the role of the hero or warrior, who does battle with the dragon in order to bring it under his will. This is a great struggle, and success is not guaranteed, but if one is able to control the primordial power of the life force, the treasure (or the maiden, which in the male psyche amounts to the same thing), is obtained. This is tantamount to attaining a higher level of consciousness -- in its highest form it constitutes "enlightenment." The symbolism of all of the hexagrams works on many different levels, and this is especially true of the first two, which must be studied together for a full comprehension of each. (Kabbalists, for example, will recognize in these two figures the same forces found in Chokmah and Binah on the Tree of Life.) For the purposes of this comparison it must be noted that the first hexagram symbolizes Heaven, and the second symbolizes Earth: Force and Form. (As consciousness is to the body it inhabits, so Force is to Form and Heaven to Earth.) Form is magnetic, or "negative" in polarity, and Force is dynamic, or "positive." In esoteric symbolism "Heaven" does not mean the universe above us -- it means the consciousness within us. This polarity is also reflected in the relationship between the ego and the Self -- in a properly regulated psyche, the ego is always magnetic to the dynamic Self. There is an invisible universe within the visible one, a world of causes within the world of effects. There is force within matter, and the two are one, and are dependent for their existence on a third, which is the mysterious cause of their existence. There is a world of soul within a world of matter, and the two are one, and caused by the world of spirit. F. Hartmann -- Paracelsus: Life and Prophecies SUGGESTIONS FOR MEDITATION To get a fuller grasp of the numinously beautiful structure of the I Ching and the transcendent reality which it reflects, one should meditate on each of the hexagrams as often as possible -- one can never exhaust their meaning. The first two hexagrams (because they are the "cosmic parents" of all the others), are especially rich in their associations. Here are a few suggestions for meditation: 1. Compare and contrast the general ideas in the first three hexagrams, noting how the third is a logical progression of the first two. 2. The Confucian commentary on The Dynamic is particularly rich in meaning. Read it over and over again -- it contains the principles of the Work as outlined in more detail in the other hexagrams. Compare the sixth sentence with the ideas in hexagram number twenty, Contemplation. 3. Compare the first two hexagrams with hexagram number eleven, Harmony, and number twelve, Divorcement, noting the implications of the symbolism in terms of the proper management of the Work.

Wenyan (文言) — Commentary on the Words of the Text

1. The Four Virtues

Philologist
“Originating” is the chief of goodness; “penetrating” is the gathering of the excellent; “beneficial” is the harmonizing of rightness; “correct and firm” is the backbone of affairs. The noble one who embodies benevolence can lead others, who gathers the fine can accord with ritual, who benefits beings can harmonize duty, and who is steadfast can manage affairs. Therefore Qian is said to be originating, penetrating, beneficial, and correct.
Commentator
The Wenyan reads yuan-heng-li-zhen as four ethical powers. Qian is not only cosmic force: it is humane leadership, fitting order, public benefit, and steady integrity.

2. Hidden Dragon

Philologist
The “hidden dragon” is one whose dragon-virtue lies concealed. He is not changed by the age and does not make a name; he withdraws from the world without distress and, when unapproved, is not embittered. When it is fitting he acts; when troubled he withdraws. Firm indeed and impossible to uproot: this is the hidden dragon.
Commentator
The first line praises inner power that refuses premature display. True worth can remain unrecognized, keep its center, and wait without resentment.

3. Dragon in the Field

Philologist
The dragon seen in the field is dragon-virtue that is correct and centered. Its ordinary words are trustworthy and its ordinary conduct careful; it shuts out crookedness and preserves sincerity, improves the age without boasting, and broadens virtue until it transforms. This is the virtue of a ruler.
Commentator
The second line shows power appearing openly but still without self-advertisement. Its authority comes from sincerity and exact balance, not from display.

4. Vigilant Diligence

Philologist
The noble one advances virtue and cultivates work. Loyalty and trust are what advance virtue; refining one’s words and establishing sincerity are what lodge one’s work. Knowing where arrival lies and arriving there, one can share in incipience; knowing where the end lies and ending there, one can preserve rightness. Therefore, though in a high place he is not proud, and though in a low place he is not anxious; all day he is active and, because of the time, watchful, so though in danger he is without blame.
Commentator
The third line stands between low and high, so it demands disciplined self-cultivation and sharp timing. Real progress comes from fidelity, truthful speech, and alertness at the edge of danger.

5. Leaping from the Abyss

Philologist
Above and below there is no constancy, yet it is not for the sake of perversity. Advancing and retreating lack fixity, yet it is not a departure from the group. The noble one advances virtue and cultivates work because he wishes to seize the proper time; therefore there is no blame.
Commentator
The fourth line is unstable in placement but not morally unstable. Its uncertainty is the testing of timing, not reckless wandering.

6. Flying Dragon in Heaven

Philologist
Like sound responds to like sound; like qi seeks like qi. Water flows toward wetness, fire goes toward dryness, clouds follow the dragon, and wind follows the tiger. When the sage arises, the myriad things behold him. What is rooted in Heaven tends upward; what is rooted in Earth tends downward; each follows its own kind.
Commentator
The fifth line describes resonance. Great power at the center of the world draws matching forces to itself as naturally as cloud joins dragon and wind joins tiger.

7. Overreaching Dragon

Philologist
To be noble and yet without position, high and yet without people, with worthy men below but no support—therefore movement brings regret.
Commentator
The top line is not condemned for strength alone but for isolated strength. Height without a real base or assistants turns action into error.

8. Sequence in Human Affairs

Philologist
“Hidden dragon: do not use” means it is below. “Seeing the dragon in the field” means it abides in its time. “All day active” means acting in affairs. “Perhaps leaping in the abyss” means testing oneself. “Flying dragon in Heaven” means governing above. “Overreaching dragon has regret” is the disaster of exhaustion. Qian’s “Use Nine” means all under Heaven is governed.
Commentator
This paragraph gives a political reading of the six lines: latency, emergence, labor, self-testing, rule, and excess. “Use Nine” gathers them into ideal order without egoistic leadership.

9. Sequence in Cosmic Terms

Philologist
“Hidden dragon: do not use” means yang qi is stored away. “Seeing the dragon in the field” means civilization is bright under Heaven. “All day active” means moving together with the time. “Perhaps leaping in the abyss” means the way of Qian is transforming. “Flying dragon in Heaven” means taking one’s place in heavenly virtue. “Overreaching dragon has regret” means reaching the time’s utmost. Qian’s “Use Nine” means seeing Heaven’s rule.
Commentator
The same six lines are now read cosmologically. They trace yang from concealment through flourishing to extremity, and “Use Nine” reveals the norm of Heaven itself.

10. Qian as Beginning

Philologist
In Qian, “originating” is that which begins and thereby penetrates; “beneficial and correct” is its nature and feeling. In beginning, Qian can beautify benefit and benefit all under Heaven; it does not spell out what it benefits—great indeed!
Commentator
Qian’s power is generous because it does not pursue a narrow advantage. Its nature is to initiate life and let benefit spread of itself.

11. Greatness of Qian

Philologist
Great indeed is Qian: hard, vigorous, centered, and correct, pure and refined. The six lines unfold it and connect broadly with all conditions. “Timely riding the six dragons” is thereby “driving Heaven”; “clouds move and rain is bestowed,” and the world is level. The noble one takes accomplished virtue as conduct, conduct visible day by day. “Hidden” means concealed and not yet seen, acting yet not yet accomplished; therefore the noble one does not use it.
Commentator
This is the grand summary. Qian is pure creative order, expressed through the six lines as stages of action, but it still insists that what is not yet ripe must remain unused.

12. Learning, Questioning, Dwelling, Acting

Philologist
The noble one learns in order to gather, asks in order to discriminate, dwells in breadth, and acts through benevolence. Therefore the Changes says, “Seeing the dragon in the field: beneficial to see the great person.” This is ruler-virtue.
Commentator
The field-dragon is not raw talent alone. It is formed by study, inquiry, generosity, and humane action.

13. Nine in the Third Place

Philologist
The third nine doubles hardness but is not centered; above, it is not in Heaven, and below, it is not in the field. Therefore it is “active, active,” and because of the time it is “watchful.” Though in danger, it is without blame.
Commentator
Line three is suspended between stations. Its safety lies in vigilance, because it has strength without settled position.

14. Nine in the Fourth Place

Philologist
The fourth nine doubles hardness but is not centered; above, it is not in Heaven, below, it is not in the field, and in the middle it is not among men. Therefore the text says “perhaps.” “Perhaps” means doubt; therefore there is no blame.
Commentator
Line four is transitional and uncertain. Its saving virtue is hesitation: not knowing yet is better than forcing the next step.

15. The Great Person

Philologist
The “great person” matches Heaven and Earth in virtue, the sun and moon in brightness, the four seasons in order, and spirits and gods in fortune and misfortune. Going before Heaven, Heaven does not oppose; following Heaven, he reveres Heaven’s times. If even Heaven does not oppose him, how much less men? How much less spirits and gods?
Commentator
The great person is not merely influential. He is perfectly aligned with cosmic order, so human and spiritual realms answer to him naturally.

16. Knowing Advance and Retreat

Philologist
“Overreaching” means knowing advance but not retreat, knowing preservation but not loss, knowing gain but not ruin. Is this not only the sage? The one who knows advance and retreat, preservation and ruin, yet does not lose the correct mean—only the sage can do this.
Commentator
The final lesson is not to reject advance but to know its limit. Only wisdom that can yield at the right moment keeps strength from turning into disaster.

Jing Fang's Yi Zhuan (京氏易傳)

1. Pure Yang and Heaven

Philologist
Qian is pure yang in operation; its image matches Heaven and it belongs to metal. Together with Kun it forms the flying and hidden counterpart and occupies the governing world-position.
Commentator
Jing Fang begins by locating Qian in a correlative system: all-yang, heavenly, metallic, and paired oppositely with Kun.

2. Use Nine and the Third Line

Philologist
The Changes says, “Use Nine: seeing a flock of dragons without a head is auspicious.” The third nine corresponds to the Three Dukes and resembles the anxious vigilance of “all day active, watchful at dusk.”
Commentator
He ties Qian’s special “Use Nine” to rulership without ego, and reads the third line as the tense responsibility of high ministers.

3. Stem-and-Branch Calculation

Philologist
Jia and Ren match the outer and inner two images. The accumulated calculation begins with Ji-Si fire and reaches Wu-Chen earth, then circles back again.
Commentator
This is Jing Fang’s numerological calendar: stems, branches, and elemental tallies cycle through Qian and return to their beginning.

4. Planets and Lodges

Philologist
Among the five planets, its assigned place begins with the Stabilizing Star; among the lodges, its assigned place begins with Shen at Ren-Xu.
Commentator
Qian is also mapped onto astronomy, receiving a starting planet and stellar station inside the same correlative grid.

5. From Hidden Dragon to Overreaching

Philologist
With the month Zi the hidden dragon begins; with the month Si it reaches the limit and governs the overreaching station.
Commentator
The yearly cycle restages the line sequence: hidden energy rises from winter and peaks in late spring, where excess begins.

6. Human and Animal Correspondences

Philologist
In human affairs it corresponds to the head, to ruler, and to father; among creatures it is horse and dragon. It distributes the five phases and apportions the six places.
Commentator
Here Qian becomes rank, kinship, body-part, and emblematic animal, then spreads its force through the six line-positions.

7. The Northwest Quarter

Philologist
It dwells in the northwest division, the place where yin and yang contend. The Changes says, “They battled in Qian.”
Commentator
Its spatial home is the northwest, a borderland of contest where the opposing forces meet head-on.

8. Six Positions and Four Images

Philologist
Heaven has six positions; Earth has six qi. Six images contain six enclosures, and the four images divide the myriad things. Yin and yang do not err; rising and descending have their grades.
Commentator
Jing Fang lays out a fully ordered cosmos: numbered stations, breaths, and images govern the patterned movement of all things.

9. Omens and Social Roles

Philologist
The auspicious and inauspicious of human affairs appear through these images, and creation and transformation divide by presence and absence. The six positions are pure yang, yet the yin image is within. Yang is ruler and yin minister; yang is the people and yin affairs. Solidity and openness, brightness and darkness: thus yin and yang can be known.
Commentator
Omens are readable because social roles and cosmic polarities mirror one another. Even pure Qian already contains the seed-form of its opposite.

10. Elemental Placements and the Return of Yin

Philologist
When water matches its position it is blessing and virtue; when wood enters the land of metal it becomes treasure; when earth approaches the inner image it stands for parents; when fire comes to the fourth and upper places it is suspect as an opposing force; when metal enters the land of metal, wood gradually wanes. In ancestral-temple reckoning the upper establishment is Xu-Hai: this is Qian’s proper station. When yang reaches the utmost, yin is born; the eight trigrams follow this rule.
Commentator
The close is a compact book of correspondences. Each phase behaves differently by position, and Qian ends with the core law of change: at the height of yang, yin begins to emerge.

Forest of Changes (焦氏易林) — All 64 Line Derivatives

All 64 Transformations

1. Qian → The Dynamic (乾之乾)

Philologist
Verse: Climbing a stony slope on the road, one speaks in foreign stammers; the interpreter is mute and deaf, so the route cannot be opened. Petitions do not go through and sought affairs achieve nothing. Commentary: Qian signifies road, ascent, and mountain, hence the rocky slope; it also governs the northwest, hence “foreign speech.” In pure Qian the three sons are absent, so there is no voice, ear, road, or petitioning image, producing muteness, deafness, blockage, and fruitless requests. Pure yang meeting pure yang chokes communication.
Commentator
Verse: Everything is uphill and blocked. Commentary: Doubled Qian is all hard ascent with no mediating trigrams, so speech, hearing, passage, and appeal all fail.

2. Qian → The Receptive (乾之坤)

Philologist
Verse: Disaster is invited and the sting comes, harming my state; sickness lies in the hands and feet, and there is no rest. Commentary: When Qian changes to Kun it draws in yin; Kun gives calamity, poison, injury, and the realm itself. The hand-image of Gen and foot-image of Zhen are lost in pure Kun, so the limbs are hurt and there is no ease.
Commentator
Verse: The omen is affliction spreading through the body politic. Commentary: Pure Kun here means passive suffering—damage reaches both state and limbs, and nothing finds repose.

3. Qian → Difficulty at the Beginning (乾之屯)

Philologist
Verse: A lone yang grows over-proud, full of resentments and confusions; the carriage overturns and its canopy is lost, the body constantly fearful. Only then is its wish attained, male and female following one another. Commentary: Kan is isolation, worry, and doubt, so the verse speaks of loneliness and alarm; Gen is the canopy and Zhen the carriage, which Kan breaks. Yet the corresponding yin-yang partners in the figure also explain the final pairing of male and female.
Commentator
Verse: Isolated force first collapses into anxiety and wreckage, then at last finds its mate. Commentary: The trigrams move from solitary hardness to proper pairing.

4. Qian → Youthful Folly (乾之蒙)

Philologist
Verse: Cuckoo and turtledove are single-minded and without fault; the noble one takes them as model and long receives fortunate blessing. Commentary: Gen is bird and stillness, hence the faithful birds and singleness; Gen is also the noble one, Kan the rule, and Zhen the blessing.
Commentator
Verse: Steady fidelity brings no blame. Commentary: The bird-images praise focused constancy, which the gentleman imitates and is blessed for.

Philologist
Verse: The eye twitches and the foot stirs; joy comes as wished, and the whole household receives favor. Commentary: Mutual Li gives the eye; movement itself gives the twitch. Qian signifies joy and favor, while Kan is the official chamber or household, so the omen becomes a welcome sign for the family.
Commentator
Verse: A small bodily sign foretells good news. Commentary: The trigrams turn a twitch into an omen of favor arriving at the house.

6. Qian → Conflict (乾之訟)

Philologist
Verse: A worn-out horse climbs the mountain; all water-springs are cut off. Throat scorches, lips dry, and the tongue cannot speak. Commentary: The annotator glosses Qian here as dragon-horse and mountain; Kan below is exhausted while inner fire and sun dry up the springs. The inverted Dui gives throat, tongue, and lips parched beside fire, so speech fails.
Commentator
Verse: The climb is dry and punishing. Commentary: Upper force and lower depletion leave no water, so even speech is burned away.

Philologist
Verse: Granaries and storehouses are full; it is fit to plant millet and panicled millet. The state is wealthy and the people multiply. Commentary: Kun is belly and sack, hence granaries; its multitude yields fullness and abundance. Zhen gives the grain crops, while Kun gives the year and the people, making this a harvest-and-population blessing.
Commentator
Verse: It is a rich agricultural omen. Commentary: Earth stores, thunder sprouts, and abundance fills both barns and population.

8. Qian → Holding Together (乾之比)

Philologist
Verse: At midnight the dog barks; a thief is outside the wall. With divine aid, all are dispersed and depart. Commentary: Kun and Kan both signify night, Kan its middle watch. Gen is the dog and wall, Kan the thief outside; hidden Qian gives spirit and Gen gives brightness, so the danger scatters away.
Commentator
Verse: Danger comes near but does not enter. Commentary: The guarding images wake in time, and unseen protection drives the thieves off.

9. Qian → Small Taming (乾之小畜)

Philologist
Verse: Grasping the Dipper and turning the pivot, one follows Heaven without sorrow and dwells together with joy. Commentary: Gen gives the stars—especially the Dipper—while Kan is the pivot. Qian moves in accord with Heaven; with Kan hidden, worry disappears, and Zhen plus Gen provide joy and secure dwelling.
Commentator
Verse: This is an image of cosmic alignment. Commentary: By taking the heavenly pivot correctly, one lives at ease and in harmony.

10. Qian → Treading (乾之履)

Philologist
Verse: Empty fists clasped, one falls to the ground and rises again. Wealth grows luxuriantly; delight has no end. Commentary: Gen gives fist and hand, Kun the ground below, and Zhen the act of rising again. Kun then becomes plenty and Zhen delight, so the image turns from collapse to recovery and abundance.
Commentator
Verse: A fall becomes a recovery. Commentary: The same trigrams that show dropping to earth also show springing back into prosperity.

11. Qian → Peace (乾之泰)

Philologist
Verse: No wind and no rain; the white sun shines bright. It is good to go out driving and find the broad road clear. Commentary: Hidden Xun removes wind and half-Kan removes rain. Zhen gives whiteness, departure, charioting, and the great road, while Qian supplies the sun; hence a clear day and unobstructed travel.
Commentator
Verse: Conditions are perfectly open. Commentary: With storm-signs absent, the trigrams describe a bright day and a free road.

12. Qian → Standstill (乾之否)

Philologist
Verse: Bearing the sun’s crystalline light, one yokes six dragons. Salary and mandate reach Heaven, and one is enfeoffed as king of Yan. Commentary: Kun bears the sun while Qian is the sun above; hidden Zhen gives dragons and the chariot-team, and Qian’s number is six. Qian also gives emolument, Xun the mandate, Dui Yan, and hidden Zhen the kingly rank.
Commentator
Verse: This is a triumphal, exalted image. Commentary: Solar brilliance, dragon-team, and heavenly mandate culminate in princely investiture.

13. Qian → Fellowship with Men (乾之同人)

Philologist
Verse: The child cries for food; the mother goes seeking it. Returning, she sees an empty nest and blames my long sigh. Commentary: Zhen gives child, crying, feeding, and asking; Kun is the mother going out, and Gen the nest made empty by Kun’s hollowness. Zhen also gives the long sigh, while opposed Dui mouths yield blame or scolding.
Commentator
Verse: Need and absence frame the scene. Commentary: The household images show a mother seeking provision only to meet emptiness and complaint.

14. Qian → Great Possession (乾之大有)

Philologist
Verse: What the High Lord engenders is daily completed by blessing and protection. Cultivate virtue and practice beneficence; be happy, secure, and at peace. Commentary: Qian supplies the High Lord, blessing, virtue, and beneficence; the paired inverse image adds joy and Kun-like repose. The omen is one of heavenly favor realized through moral conduct.
Commentator
Verse: Heaven’s gift ripens through virtue. Commentary: The poem links abundance not to force alone but to the steady practice of goodness.

Philologist

Verse: It is a grueling transport through rough terrain. Commentary: The trigrams pile hazard upon hazard—ravine, stones, broken cart, and injured foot.

Commentator

31. Qian → Influence (乾之咸)

Philologist
Verse: Three men seek oranges and instead find a cinnabar cave. The woman becomes honored and wealthy, with a hundred yi of gold. Commentary: Mutual Qian gives the men and the fruit they seek; Gen gives the seeking, and Dui the cave, reddened by Qian into a cinnabar cave. Xun is the woman; supported by Qian she becomes wealthy, and hidden Kun plus Qian provide the yellow gold in abundance.
Commentator
Verse: What is sought is exceeded by what is found. Commentary: The trigram search for fruit unexpectedly opens into wealth, treasure, and female prosperity.

32. Qian → Duration (乾之恆)

Philologist
Verse: Eastern hills and western peaks meet and eat together; a hundred households escort and follow, bringing blessing to completion. Commentary: Mutual Gen gives the mountains, Zhen the east, Dui the west. Kun makes the meeting, doubled Zhen the shared eating and the many followers, and Gen the finished blessing.
Commentator
Verse: It is a scene of lasting alliance. Commentary: Separated heights are joined, and that meeting spreads benefit through many households.

33. Qian → Retreat (乾之遯)

Philologist
Verse: A weak cock without spurs fights with a magpie; its wings break, its eyes are blinded, and it is wounded by a dove. Commentary: Xun gives the fowl, but the hidden Zhen that would provide spurs is absent. Gen gives magpie and fighting, inverted Zhen broken wings, Li damaged sight, and another Gen-bird the final wound.
Commentator
Verse: This is a bad fight for a weak bird. Commentary: The trigrams strip away the cock’s natural equipment, so combat ends in injury.

34. Qian → Great Power (乾之大壯)

Philologist
Verse: The crack grows and the great wall collapses; worms eat the timber and many beams break. Wolves and tigers govern, Heaven sends punishment; Zhao Gao killed at Wangyi, and Hu Hai thereby died. Commentary: Xun gives withdrawal, walls, wood-borers, and rotted timber; Kun multiplies the damage. Gen gives wolves and tigers, Kun the regime and the punishment, and the same images are extended into the historical warning of Zhao Gao’s murder of the Second Emperor.
Commentator
Verse: Strength has become destructive excess. Commentary: Structural decay in wall and timber widens into political ruin and dynastic murder.

35. Qian → Progress (乾之晉)

Philologist
Verse: Three fools run together, losing the road and straying from the Way. Confused and not knowing how to return, they instead enter the mouth of trouble. Commentary: Kun gives stupidity and the triad, mutual Gen the roads, and Kan confusion. Zhen would return, but the yang does not turn back; Kan becomes trouble and a Dui-shaped mouth into which they wander.
Commentator
Verse: What looks like movement is really misdirection. Commentary: The trigrams show progress gone wrong—running forward into danger because one cannot turn back.

36. Qian → Darkening of the Light (乾之明夷)

Philologist
Verse: Bow and arrows are all drawn; the pellet-pouch snaps the string. The shot is released but does not reach; on the road one meets harm and trouble. Commentary: Mutual Kan gives bow, arrows, and pellet; Zhen gives the drawing and the firing, but Kan breaks the string. Kun blocks the shot, and Kun again makes the roadside harm.
Commentator
Verse: The weapon is ready but fails at the crucial moment. Commentary: Broken mechanism turns intent into frustrated danger.

37. Qian → The Family (乾之家人)

Philologist
Verse: Three women seek a husband, waiting by the mountain corner. They do not see the double gate and sigh in long sorrow. Commentary: Two Li forms and one Xun form provide the three women, while Kan is the absent husband. Gen gives gate, mountain, and waiting stillness; because Kan is hidden he does not appear, leaving only longing and lament.
Commentator
Verse: Domestic desire is suspended in absence. Commentary: The trigrams make a patient, sorrowing watch for one who does not come.

38. Qian → Opposition (乾之睽)

Philologist
Verse: Fierce drought and blazing heat damage the grain. Reapers have no food and the plowman sighs. Commentary: Doubled Li makes drought, while Zhen gives the crops; broken half-forms destroy them. Dui would feed, but Kan loses the food, and Zhen plus a Dui mouth make the farmer’s sigh.
Commentator
Verse: The field is scorched before it can nourish. Commentary: The fiery opposition of the trigrams consumes both crop and livelihood.

39. Qian → Obstruction (乾之蹇)

Philologist
Verse: Riding a piglet and chasing sheep, one never sees what one hopes for. Passing by-paths through the tiger’s den, one loses ram and lamb alike. Commentary: Half Zhen gives riding and pursuit, hidden Dui the sheep, but Kan conceals what is sought. Gen gives the path, tiger, and lair, and with Dui hidden the flock is lost.
Commentator
Verse: The pursuit leads only into danger. Commentary: The obstructing trigrams conceal the quarry and drive the seeker into a predatory place.

40. Qian → Release (乾之解)

Philologist
Verse: Dark, dim, and murky words are passed along in mutual entanglement and error. Ghosts and spirits lodge there; who knows where they lie? Commentary: Kan gives darkness and the underworld, Zhen speech, and opposing Dui mouths the crossed-up transmission. Kan also gives ghosts, lodgings, and concealment, so the place becomes uncanny and unknowable.
Commentator
Verse: Communication becomes haunted confusion. Commentary: Because every mouth speaks across another, the whole scene turns spectral and obscure.

41. Qian → Decrease (乾之損)

Philologist
Verse: Ji and Jiang are auspicious and gentle; two people share a meal and discuss benevolence and blessing, thereby securing the royal house. Commentary: Mutual Zhen gives the Zhou-clan Ji and the humane discourse; hidden Xun supplies Jiang of Qi. Dui gives the pair and the meal, Zhen the speech, and Gen the royal house settled in peace.
Commentator
Verse: Good alliance strengthens the house. Commentary: The trigrams make marriage, conversation, and shared food serve political stability.

42. Qian → Increase (乾之益)

Philologist
Verse: A duke’s grandson drives a black horse and carries betrothal gifts to eastern Qi. At Yanling he delights Zichan and leaves a fine linen robe for the younger man. Commentary: Gen gives the grandson, Zhen the duke, horse, chariot, and eastern movement; Xun marks Qi. Gen also becomes Yanling, Zhen delight and Zichan, and Kun with Zhen provide the garment offered as a gift.
Commentator
Verse: Travel, diplomacy, and gift exchange all prosper. Commentary: The commentary turns the trigrams into an elegant record of noble approach and reciprocal courtesy.

43. Qian → Breakthrough (乾之夬)

Philologist
Verse: At the ruin of Guzhu, the wife is lost and the husband dead; hurt among caltrops, she does not see her fair spouse. Eastern-Quarter Tang Jiang—through her, Master Wu perished. Commentary: The annotator reads this through the Zuo Commentary story of Cui Zhu and Tang Jiang. Kun makes the ruined waste and widow-like loss, Dui the older feminine image, hidden Gen the absent husband, and the caltrop motif recalls the disastrous omen from Hexagram 47.
Commentator
Verse: The poem is an omen of sexual-political disaster. Commentary: Broken household imagery and the Tang Jiang allusion make desire inseparable from ruin.

44. Qian → Coming to Meet (乾之姤)

Philologist
Verse: Humane government is not violent; phoenixes come to roost. The four seasons keep their proper order and the people rest in their place. Commentary: Zhen gives humane rule and the four seasonal markers, while Kun gives cultured plumage, the people, and their settled security. The omen is of good order answering to virtue.
Commentator
Verse: Right rule draws auspicious creatures. Commentary: Phoenix, season, and stable populace all signal a polity in harmony.

45. Qian → Gathering (乾之萃)

Philologist
Verse: Bearing is poor and strength is thin; the weak jade-horse is fearful and timid. Like a hedgehog seeing a magpie, it does not dare resist. Commentary: Kun’s softness makes weakness and poor capacity; a great Kan within makes fear. Xun gives the hedgehog, Gen the magpie, and the weak Kun body cannot stand against it.
Commentator
Verse: This is the picture of timidity before a sharper presence. Commentary: The trigrams assemble weakness, fear, and defensive curling-in rather than bold resistance.

46. Qian → Pushing Upward (乾之升)

Philologist
Verse: The Marquis of Wei travels east and is bewitched by the young Ji. I lose my father and mother; long astray, he does not return. Commentary: Zhen gives guards, lords, and eastern travel; a great Kan gives delusion and Dui the young lady. Zhen is father, Kun mother and “myself”; because Kun dies and Zhen goes away, the parents are lost and the wanderer does not come back.
Commentator
Verse: Upward movement becomes exile through desire. Commentary: The trigrams turn noble travel into seduction, estrangement, and familial loss.

47. Qian → Oppression (乾之困)

Philologist
Verse: What is said face to face is better than my wall. Joyfully firm and solid, it can keep one long at peace. Commentary: The line plays on opposed mouths speaking to each other; the hexagram already says “words are not trusted.” Xun gives the wall, but trapped Kan makes one prefer the security of one’s own enclosure; Dui joy with hidden Gen solidity gives lasting safety.
Commentator
Verse: In distress, enclosure can become refuge. Commentary: The commentary shifts from doubtful speech to the reassuring firmness of a defended wall.

48. Qian → The Well (乾之井)

Philologist
Verse: A yue-bird calls on Mount Qi and a turtle answers from the dark abyss. Male and female join essences, the ten thousand things transform and are born; King Wen brought it to completion and opened the court of Zhou. Commentary: The annotator sees answering creatures across upper and lower trigrams, then reads Kan as male essence and Li as female form. Zhen brings forth life, kingship, and Zhou, while Gen gives completion and the court.
Commentator
Verse: Resonance becomes generation. Commentary: Call and response across the trigrams expand into sexual union, creation, and the founding of Zhou.

49. Qian → Revolution (乾之革)

Philologist
Verse: Dark-yellow collapse and sickness; travelers are worn out with fatigue. Laborers grow haggard and, beyond the proper time, do not return. Commentary: Mutual Zhen gives the “dark-yellow” pallor named in the Odes, while Kan makes illness and toil. Zhen is the traveler, Kan the laboring man, and Gen the time that is passed over without return.
Commentator
Verse: Weariness has gone beyond the normal term. Commentary: Sickly color, labor, and delay combine into an omen of prolonged exhaustion.

50. Qian → The Cauldron (乾之鼎)

Philologist
Verse: Weak feet and cut heels make it unfavorable to go out the door. Merchants gain no profit, and broken losses become trouble. Commentary: Lower Xun weakens the feet while hidden Zhen disappears, echoing the Ding first line’s overturned toes. Hidden Gen gives the door, Xun the market and profit, but broken Dui and a great Kan turn commerce into loss and trouble.
Commentator
Verse: Mobility and trade both fail. Commentary: The trigrams remove bodily footing first, then extend that failure into the marketplace.

51. Qian → Arousing Thunder (乾之震)

Philologist
Verse: A hanging badger is eaten for nothing, dwelling where it has no proper place. One loses carriage and house; leaving the seat, one moves elsewhere. Commentary: The phrase comes from the Odes about eating without earning. Mutual Gen gives the badger, hidden Xun the rope that hangs it, Zhen food and whiteness; overturned Gen removes proper dwelling, house, seat, and stability, while Kan steals away the carriage.
Commentator
Verse: Unearned consumption ends in displacement. Commentary: Because the dwelling-images overturn, carriage, house, and seat are all lost.

52. Qian → Keeping Still (乾之艮)

Philologist
Verse: The people are fearful and the city poor; traitors lie hidden. Great raiders arrive, enter my outer walls, and wife and children are taken captive. Commentary: Gen is the city-wall, Kan the frightened people, traps, and bandits, with hidden Xun providing the entry. Hidden Dui gives the wife, Zhen the children, and Gen’s immobilization becomes capture.
Commentator
Verse: Defensive stillness has failed. Commentary: The city image remains, but fear and hidden infiltration let the enemy seize the household.

53. Qian → Gradual Progress (乾之漸)

Philologist
Verse: Yang lowers its head and yin raises hers. Water becomes disaster and injures what is at my feet; advance is unfavorable and giving birth is hard. Commentary: Kan supplies head and water, but hidden below it sinks downward; Li, a yin trigram, rises above it. Overturned Zhen harms what is underfoot and blocks both advance and childbirth.
Commentator
Verse: The balance of high and low is reversed. Commentary: Because the trigrams oppose proper growth, movement and reproduction both meet obstruction.

54. Qian → The Marrying Maiden (乾之歸妹)

Philologist
Verse: Back to back toward the north, they hate one another; mind and intention differ, like fire and metal. Commentary: Hidden Gen gives the backs, Kan the north, resentment, and intention; Li gives fire and hidden Gen metal. The trigrams themselves make irreconcilable opposition.
Commentator
Verse: The pair does not truly join. Commentary: Direction, feeling, and elemental quality all run against one another.

55. Qian → Abundance (乾之豐)

Philologist
Verse: The palace of the Supreme Subtlety is where the Yellow Emperor properly stands. Encircling screens and guards surround it; one cannot enter, and so it remains ever safe and without trouble. Commentary: Li gives stars and the celestial palace, Zhen the emperor and the Xuanyuan star, hidden Kan the chambers. Zhen also makes the surrounding hedges and guards, while Xun’s blocked entry keeps the place secure.
Commentator
Verse: It is an image of a protected celestial court. Commentary: Star-palace, emperor, and layered defenses combine into untouchable security.

56. Qian → The Wanderer (乾之旅)

Philologist
Verse: Silkworm-colored sacrificial cattle are reverently offered to ghosts and spirits. The spirits delight in food and drink and bestow blessing and many grandsons. Commentary: Xun gives the cocoon-like color, Gen chestnuts, and Li the sacrificial ox; the ritual phrase comes from classic liturgy. Hidden Kan gives ghosts, Zhen gods and food, Gen descendants, and Kan multitude, hence many grandsons.
Commentator
Verse: This is a favorable sacrificial omen. Commentary: Correct offerings bring a response of fecund blessing down the family line.

57. Qian → The Gentle Wind (乾之巽)

Philologist
Verse: Going out the gate one meets evil, living with calamity and resentment. They stab and strike one another, injuring my fingertips. Commentary: Gen gives the gate and hand, Kan evil, trouble, resentment, and brokenness. The reversed Gen forms mutual striking, so the harm lands directly on the hands.
Commentator
Verse: Encounter turns quickly into mutual wounding. Commentary: The trigrams localize the quarrel at the threshold and in the hands themselves.

58. Qian → The Joyous Lake (乾之兌)

Philologist
Verse: The yì-birds fly back in midair; undertakings do not advance, and the crowd breaks into disorder. Commentary: The annotator connects this to the omen of the “six yì birds retreating in flight” before the rout of the state of Song. Hidden Gen gives the bird, Xun retreat, and mutual Zhen the undertaking and the people thrown into disorder.
Commentator
Verse: Retreat in the sky foretells failure below. Commentary: Because the bird-image turns backward, public action collapses into confusion.

59. Qian → Dispersion (乾之渙)

Philologist
Verse: The lame and crippled follow one another; by sunset the ox is spent. The journey falls behind and declines; profit is lost and the female is lost as well. Commentary: Kan makes lameness and weariness, Xun compliant following, Gen the sun, dusk, and ox. Xun also governs gain and the female, but Kan takes both away.
Commentator
Verse: Attrition overtakes both travel and livelihood. Commentary: Day wanes, the beast tires, and the hexagram disperses profit, pace, and mate alike.

60. Qian → Limitation (乾之節)

Philologist
Verse: Dragon horns and a broad brow bring one to the rank of duke or minister. Hereditary emolument lasts long, and rising or moving ends in peace. Commentary: Zhen gives the dragon and public rank, Gen the horns, and the broad brow is treated as a noble physiognomic sign. Kan gives salary, Gen duration and security, Zhen rise and movement.
Commentator
Verse: The signs are those of noble advancement. Commentary: Auspicious bodily marks are read together with stable office and enduring stipend.

61. Qian → Inner Truth (乾之中孚)

Philologist
Verse: Shun raises up Great Yu in the wilds of Shiyi. Summoned to the royal court, he joins in ordering the waters and the soil. Commentary: Zhen gives the emperor figures Shun and Yu; Gen gives stone and wilderness. Zhen again goes to courtly summons, Gen gives the court and the bowing hands, while a great Kan supplies water and earth to be governed.
Commentator
Verse: Hidden worth is recognized and employed. Commentary: The trigrams frame Yu’s rise as a summons from wilderness into world-ordering service.

62. Qian → Small Exceeding (乾之小過)

Philologist
Verse: With the wind one sets fire, and reeds and mugwort die together. Three harms gather in the house, and the younger son is wounded. Commentary: Xun is wind and grasses, Gen fire; joined together they make a wind-driven blaze. Gen gives the house and the younger son, Kan the harms within, and broken Dui the injury.
Commentator
Verse: Small excess becomes domestic damage. Commentary: What begins as wind and flame ends by striking the household’s vulnerable member.

63. Qian → After Completion (乾之既濟)

Philologist
Verse: Catalpa grows on Jing Mountain, and command is given to the master craftsman. Robes are stripped away; summer burns and winter freezes. Hungry and withered, one wins no one’s pity. Commentary: Repeated Zhen forms trees and garments; half Gen makes the mountain and half Xun command and craftsmanship. Kan and Li yield winter cold and summer heat, while emptied Li and Kan make hunger, desiccation, and the crowd’s indifference.
Commentator
Verse: Skill and materials are present, yet the outcome is deprivation. Commentary: The trigrams move from making and clothing to exposure, seasonal extremes, and abandonment.

64. Qian → Before Completion (乾之未濟)

Philologist
Verse: A long face and great nose come to relieve my sorrow. They leave me blessed sons and a kindly wife, granting me happy joy. Commentary: Repeated Gen forms face and nose; Kan is sorrow, but Zhen brings release, blessing, sons, wife, and delight. The close turns the half-images into a domestic good ending.
Commentator
Verse: The sequence ends with relief and household blessing. Commentary: What began in blocked force closes in family happiness, showing how even unfinished transition can yield joy.
