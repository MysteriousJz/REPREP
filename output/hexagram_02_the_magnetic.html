文言 (Wenyan Commentary)

1. Softness, stillness, and timely movement

Philologist
'The Commentary on the Words' says: Kun is utmost soft, yet in movement it is hard; utmost still, yet in virtue it is square. Later it gets the lord and thus has constancy; it contains the ten thousand things and its transforming is radiant. The way of Kun is surely obedient: it bears Heaven and moves with the seasons.
Commentator
The appendix defines Kun as perfect receptivity: yielding in substance, effective in action, quiet, steady, and properly squared. It does not lead, but by carrying Heaven and moving when the time comes, it gives form and brightness to all things.

2. Frost, habit, and the slow growth of disaster

Philologist
In families that accumulate good, there is sure to be surplus blessing; in families that accumulate what is not good, there is sure to be surplus calamity. A minister killing his ruler, a son killing his father, is not the cause of one morning or one evening: what it comes from has grown gradually, because the distinction was not distinguished early. The Yi says, 'Treading frost: hard ice arrives' — this is speaking of sequence and compliance.
Commentator
Moral consequences build up over time. The first frost is a warning: if small deviations are not recognized early, they harden into full catastrophe.

3. Straight within, square without

Philologist
'Straight' is its correctness; 'square' is its righteousness. The superior man, by reverence, makes the inside straight; by righteousness, he makes the outside square. When reverence and righteousness are established, virtue is not solitary. 'Straight, square, great, and without practice nothing is unfavorable' means that he does not doubt the course of his action.
Commentator
The line is glossed ethically: inner seriousness makes one upright, outer justice gives one proper boundaries. When those two are firmly in place, action becomes spacious and unhesitating.

4. Concealed beauty and the caution of service

Philologist
Though yin has beauty, it 'contains' it in order to follow the king's affairs; it does not dare complete the work itself. This is the way of earth, the way of a wife, the way of a minister. The way of earth has 'no accomplishment' yet in another's place 'has an end.' When Heaven and Earth transform, grasses and trees grow thick; when Heaven and Earth close, worthy people hide. The Yi says, 'Tie up the sack: no blame, no praise' — this is speaking of caution.
Commentator
Receptive power serves without claiming credit. It keeps its beauty inward, helps the larger work reach completion, and knows when closed times require the worthy to withdraw into careful silence.

5. Yellow in the center

Philologist
For the superior man, 'yellow' means the center opening into pattern and principle, taking the correct position and dwelling in the substance. Beauty is within and then flows freely into the four limbs, issuing in undertakings: this is beauty at its utmost.
Commentator
Yellow is the color of centered earth. When inward order is truly centered, it spreads outward into the whole body and then into one's work, making conduct itself the fullest form of beauty.

6. Dragon-battle and the mingling of dark and yellow

Philologist
When yin is in doubt before yang, it is bound to 'battle,' because otherwise it would seem as if there were no yang. Therefore 'dragon' is named. Yet it has still not left its own kind, therefore 'blood' is named. As for 'dark and yellow,' that is the mixedness of Heaven and Earth: Heaven is dark, Earth is yellow.
Commentator
At the extreme, pure receptivity can no longer remain merely receptive, so conflict appears. The dragon image marks the presence of yang within the struggle, while the dark-yellow blood shows Heaven and Earth mixed together at the point of collision.

京氏易傳 (Jing Fang's Yi Tradition)

1. Pure yin, earth, and soil

Philologist
Kun: pure yin is in operation; its emblem matches Earth; it belongs to soil; the way of softness is luminous.
Commentator
Jing Fang opens by defining Kun as unmixed yin: earthly, earthy, and radiant precisely in its yielding mode.

2. Mutual reception with Qian

Philologist
Yin congeals and responds; it is mutually received with Qian. This is the minister attending the ruler.
Commentator
Kun is not isolated: it meets Qian in a structured pairing, and Jing Fang reads that relation politically as loyal service.

3. Yellow skirt and the central minister

Philologist
The Yi says, 'Yellow skirt, primal good fortune.' In six in the second place, the yin of the inner trigram occupies the center; the ministerial way is correct. With Qian it forms a flying-and-hidden correspondence.
Commentator
The famous second line is central, modest, and proper. For Jing Fang that is exactly the right posture of the minister who answers the ruler without seizing initiative.

4. High office, dragon-battle, and completion without claiming achievement

Philologist
The ancestral temple occupies the generation-position; the Three Dukes make the response. Yet one still does not avoid the calamity of dragons battling: there is no self-made accomplishment, but there is completion in the end.
Commentator
Even when Kun rises to the highest ministerial sphere, it remains exposed to the danger named in the top line. Its virtue is not to found things in its own name, but to carry them through.

5. From frost to hard ice

Philologist
At first six begins 'treading frost' and reaches 'hard ice.' Though yin is soft and compliant, its qi is also firm and hard: this is because it is a qi without perversity.
Commentator
Yielding does not mean weak. Kun's energy can harden from first trace to full formation, but in Jing Fang's reading that firmness is orderly rather than deviant.

6. Stem-branch reckoning

Philologist
For the founding beginning, Jiawu reaches to Jihai; in accumulated calculation, it begins from Jihai and reaches Wuxu, circling and then beginning again.
Commentator
Jing Fang next assigns Kun its technical calendrical cycle, moving through stem-branch spans that return upon themselves in repetition.

7. Planets, direction, lodges, and seasonal allotment

Philologist
Among the five planets, from its proper station it begins with Great Yin. It is the hexagram of the southwest. Among the stellar lodges, from their proper station it descends to Guiyou Metal, dividing the seasonal qi into thirty-six portions.
Commentator
Kun is then placed within correlative cosmology: lunar force, the southwest, a metal-based stellar position, and a measured share of seasonal breath.

8. Yin within which yang is present

Philologist
Within yin there is yang; qi accumulates and the myriad images arise, therefore it is called yin within yin.
Commentator
Even the most yin hexagram is not dead emptiness. Because latent yang is already present inside it, the stored breath can generate the whole world of images.

9. Signs, positions, and the division of the trigrams

Philologist
The two qi of yin and yang are where Heaven and Earth join. Human affairs, auspicious or inauspicious, are seen in their signs. The six places adapt and transform; from this the eight trigrams are apportioned.
Commentator
For Jing Fang, cosmic pattern and human fortune are read together. Change in the six places differentiates the eight trigrams and makes events legible.

10. Emptiness lodged in a yang place, and the name Yi

Philologist
Though yin is empty, when it is received into a yang place it is called solid. Rising and descending, reversing and returning, it cannot remain long in one condition. There are a thousand changes and ten thousand transformations; therefore it is called the Yi. Yi means change.
Commentator
Position alters value: even yin may count as substantial when set in a yang place. Because ascent, descent, reversal, and return never cease, the Classic is fundamentally the book of change.

11. The return of yang

Philologist
When yin reaches the limit, yang comes; when yin wanes, yang grows. In decline it retreats; in fullness it battles. The Yi says, 'Top six: dragons battle in the wild; their blood is dark and yellow.' Belonging to yang, yang stirs yin. When Kun's first six of the inner trigram changes and enters yang, it is called Zhen. Yin is full and yang slight, but this is the sense of gradual coming, therefore it is called Return. Next, descending yang enters the Earth-Thunder hexagram Return.
Commentator
The end of Kun already contains the beginning of Fu. Dragon-battle marks the crisis point where extreme yin yields to a first stirring of yang, and Jing Fang tracks that turning through the line-change that produces Zhen and then Fu.
